# The Protest That Should Have Changed the Record

## A patient-commissioned documentary indictment, July 2017–October 2026

*This manuscript examines what the medical record received, changed, omitted and made authoritative. It is a patient-commissioned argument, not a judicial finding. The account of the bedside Protest is presented as the patient's first-hand testimony. Clinical and institutional claims are distinguished from inferences about the effect of documents.*

## The eighth day

On Monday, 10 July 2017, the patient asked his neurologist why his feet had not been adequately investigated. He had been in hospital since the previous Monday. He says that four medical students were present. He recalls putting his concern directly to the consultant and asking, in substance, **“Why haven't you looked at my feet?”** He says the examination he sought did not follow. The hospital's contemporaneous account does not visibly record this objection. It is the patient's evidence, not a quotation authenticated by an institutional note. This manuscript begins with it because the question was clinically substantial before anyone began to reconstruct the history eight years later.

The hospital had not failed to find a serious disease. It had found one. Severe young-onset Parkinsonism had been recognised. Rigidity, impaired movement initiation, bradykinesia and an extraordinary gait had been examined. Neurology had observed a lower limb whose movement looked dystonic. It had recorded deformities of the feet, and it had begun treating lower-limb tension. By 10 July, an important investigation had therefore been performed. But the original relationship between the patient's painful feet, clawing toes, abnormal gait and increasing muscular tone was not comparably explained in the available evidence. The objection arose at the boundary between what the hospital had successfully established and what it had left inadequately accounted for.

That distinction matters. The patient was not asking the neurologist to abandon Parkinsonism. He was not presenting a rival genetic diagnosis. He was not insisting that every corn and callus must be caused by dystonia. He was identifying part of the clinical problem he had brought into hospital and saying that its examination remained incomplete. He did not need a copy of the referral or the specialist notes to recognise the problem. He had lived with the painful feet; he knew what he had come to have investigated. His testimony and the hospital's own records met at the same clinical point. The available documents already contained reasons to test how neurological movement, altered gait, toe posture and foot pathology related to one another.

On 29 June, the GP had written to the Medical Assessment Unit describing a patient with painful bilateral foot problems and a strikingly abnormal gait. He had first seen the patient in April. Corns and calluses were part of that history, but the picture had expanded: greater stiffness, increased tone in the limbs, difficulty initiating movement, inability to relax, clawing toes and worsening dexterity. The decline was affecting work. The GP suspected Parkinsonism or another neurological disorder and asked for urgent assessment. His referral did not divide the patient into a proven movement disorder and unrelated diseased feet. It sent a complex relationship for expert investigation. The feet were not peripheral to that request; they were among the experiences through which the progressive disorder had become visible.

The hospital's initial précis foregrounded the motor syndrome: difficulty initiating movement, increased tone, stiffness and cogwheeling. That was understandable clinical prioritisation. These were signs that demanded neurological investigation. But a summary can preserve the most recognisable features of a case while reducing the explanatory importance of others. In the movement from referral to admission framing, the painful feet and toe clawing were less prominent. Their relationship to the motor disorder did not disappear as a documented fact; it lost some of its power to define what the investigation still needed to answer. The distinction between information being available and information remaining operationally influential is central to what happened next.

The neurological examination brought the two territories close again. It recorded marked Parkinsonian dysfunction, dystonic-looking lower-limb behaviour, abnormal gait and genuine structural foot deformities. Those observations did not prove a single cause for the painful feet. Structural disease may be independent; peripheral nerve disease may coexist with Parkinsonism; pressure lesions may have several causes. But the examination supplied additional clinical reasons not to assume independence without testing it. Dystonic posture can alter the forces exerted on a foot; fixed mechanical abnormalities can also contribute to pain regardless of neurological state. Establishing where one mechanism ended and another began required more than naming the parts. The question was how they interacted in this patient.

The hospital's response was active but divided. Clonazepam was prescribed at night to relieve tension in the legs. Procyclidine and rasagiline entered the symptomatic treatment plan. Podiatry was asked to review problems with the feet. On 6 July the GP had telephoned Podiatry and recorded that the patient would be prioritised “as Parkinson's.” These are real clinical actions. They rule out an honest description of the admission as an absence of foot-related care. They do not establish that Neurology had adequately investigated how abnormal movement and foot pathology were connected. A referral to treat pressure lesions is not, without further evidence, a movement-disorders examination of why those lesions occurred, changed or remained painful.

By the eighth calendar day, the hospital had therefore reached a position in which its neurological achievement was substantial, its treatment planning had begun, and the original integrated question remained only partly reconstructed in the surviving account. Eight calendar days included a weekend; they do not imply eight uninterrupted days of consultant availability. But the chronology still matters. This was no longer the first hour of triage. An investigation was approaching discharge. The clinician had accumulated observations and could compare the GP's referral with what had been established. The patient was now raising the residual problem directly. His objection created a fresh clinical fact: the person being examined disputed the completeness of the assessment before the authoritative discharge account had finally closed.

An accountable response was possible without promising a particular diagnosis or prolonging admission indefinitely. The consultant could have examined the painful feet in their neurological context, explained why existing findings were sufficient, sought another assessment, made a specific referral or documented the patient's concern with an assigned plan for reviewing it. A diagnosis need not be certain for a discharge to be reasonable. But significant uncertainty must be correctly described, and someone must know what remains to be done. On the patient's account, no satisfactory examination followed his request. The surviving record does not visibly preserve the objection as an unresolved disagreement. The problem was not only what the doctors knew. It was what the completed document would allow later readers to know about the challenge to that knowledge.

The neurologist's handwritten interview, dated Monday, 3 July, recorded the patient becoming aware that foot pain and leg tension were not the same. That is an account of lived sensations. The typed history strengthened it into the patient's realisation that they were “not the same problem.” The final impression stated that the patient had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” the leg tension from corns, calluses and pain in the feet. These are not equivalent statements. People can experience tension and pressure pain as different without knowing whether different medical causes produced them. A corn may be a real structural lesion while neurological posturing influences the pressure that makes it painful. The handwritten distinction did not settle that question. The final attribution made the patient's own understanding appear more decisive.

Beside that attribution, the specialist impression entertained the possibility that the leg and foot symptoms, including burning, corns and calluses, had been completely separate or reflected secondary peripheral neuropathy, perhaps related in some respect to historical alcohol intake. The impression ended the causal speculation with the words “Not clear.” Uncertainty was legitimate. There was no requirement to produce certainty where the evidence could not support it. But the uncertainty was coupled with a more definite representation of the patient as strongly separating symptoms himself. The record thereby acquired an explanation under which a relationship that remained unestablished could be treated as provisionally separate. It was a coherent way of completing the account. It did not demonstrate that the clinical inquiry challenged by the patient had been completed.

This is the first documentary indictment. On the stipulated bedside account, the patient had told the neurologist the feet were insufficiently investigated. The completed account did not visibly preserve that challenge. Instead, it preserved a progressively stronger version of the patient's supposed separation of sensations, positioned next to the clinician's uncertain separate or peripheral hypothesis. The source comparison demonstrates a transformation of wording. It does not establish that the clinician deliberately transformed it to answer the Protest. The drafting dates of the typed history and foot paragraphs are not independently fixed. The DaTscan, obtained on 13 July after discharge on 11 July, was later incorporated into the completed specialist synthesis. The document plainly contains information from different dates. Its final form cannot be assumed to reproduce every thought held on 10 July. But whatever the motive or drafting sequence, its completed effect remains examinable.

The specialist account went on to make a sophisticated management plan. Parkinsonism was investigated through imaging, differential diagnosis, discussion of genetic possibilities and consideration of long-term drug risks. The clinicians were cautious about starting levodopa or dopamine agonists very young because of dyskinesia and other complications. That caution was not imaginary. Later experience would demonstrate how difficult medication trade-offs could become. The report referred to a Complex Case Meeting, nursing input and other investigations. At the formal recommendations it stated, “As per above.” This phrase was not in itself suspicious. It was the ordinary incorporation of an extensive clinical synthesis. The question was whether the unresolved relationship between neurological movement and painful feet had gained a sufficiently explicit investigative owner, or had simply become one uncertain branch within an otherwise actionable plan.

The following months resist any story of continuous neglect. Procyclidine brought substantial benefit. The patient reported extraordinary improvement in physical function. He trusted his neurologist and had good reasons to do so. His foot symptoms were not uniformly severe across the ensuing years. Treatment changed how he moved and how he experienced his body. The relationship between doctor and patient contained real clinical success. None of this resolves the documentary question. It makes that question more exact. A treatment can work while the record under which it is prescribed continues to represent an important relationship incompletely. Successful neurological care is not independent verification of every proposition embedded in its founding explanation.

In 2017 a medication interruption produced worsening tension and movement. The treating neurologist's April 2018 note later recorded the patient's report that the first rapidly returning change during procyclidine withdrawal involved the outside-left-foot corn. The clinician documented what the patient reported; he did not establish that corn tissue visibly formed in a matter of days. The physical mechanism remains uncertain. But an observation involving the precise painful-foot territory allowed a practical question to be asked. Did medication-state changes influence dystonic posturing, loading, gait or the experience of pressure pain? The record had now generated information capable of testing the original explanatory division. The fact that neurological treatment modified symptoms was not proof that every lesion was neurological. It was a reason to investigate how the mechanisms related.

The question kept returning. Primary care connected foot problems with tone, clawing and abnormal gait in 2019. In 2020 a footdrop-like movement was specifically assessed as Parkinsonian rather than accepted as evidence of true foot weakness. Later clinicians considered OFF-related pain, other peripheral explanations and medication timing. Levodopa treatment began in 2021. Later adjustments brought relief and complications, including abnormal involuntary movements. These are documented instances of actual clinical reassessment. They defeat an allegation that nothing changed or nobody reconsidered the lower limbs. The more precise criticism is that the record demonstrates these local revisions far more readily than it demonstrates a comprehensive reconstruction of the original feet–tone–gait relationship. The clinical account could absorb new findings without clearly revisiting how its founding separation was earned.

By March 2023, the history had become dangerous in another way. The GP urgently reported severe bilateral foot and lower-leg pain and said it was “causing death wish.” He expressed concern for the patient and requested review before the situation deteriorated. A movement-disorders nurse recorded profound loss of quality of life. Neurological treatment and assessment continued, and those actions must be recognised. But treating pain and assessing the safety of someone describing a death wish are not identical clinical tasks. The warning called for consideration of the meaning and immediacy of psychological risk, appropriate communication and protective follow-up. The material examined has not demonstrated a distinct, coordinated and adequately documented safety response. That does not prove that no such assessment occurred elsewhere. It means the institution should be able to identify and explain what was done.

In March 2025, controlled-release Sinemet brought the patient reported dramatic relief from severe morning foot pain. The details of its initiation require accuracy: the patient described the trial as independently undertaken, while the medical record shows a Neurology prescription dated 10 March 2025. The response did not prove that the same medication should necessarily have been used years earlier, or that every historical episode of pain had one cause. It added a powerful new observation to a long history of neurological treatment modifying the feet. On 19 March 2025 the patient sent a formal complaint alleging diagnostic anchoring, inadequate reassessment and prolonged suffering. He requested independent review, disclosure, explanation and preservation of the records. It was, in substance, the old Protest in a more developed documentary form: why was the relationship between his painful feet and neurological disease never adequately settled or re-examined?

The hospital's complaint process generated further documents. In April the Quality and Patient Safety Department described the complaint principally as an issue of clinical judgment and directed it through the relevant pathway. The treating neurologist explained the history of care, the genuine risks of medication and his recognition that Sinemet could improve the experience of pain whatever its mechanism. The relationship was represented as personally constructive, with understandable regret for the suffering described. In September, the Serious Incident Management Team reported that there had been “no deficit in the care provided.” The stated reasons emphasised the appropriateness of decisions, consideration of alternatives and the risks of prescribing Sinemet at a young age. Those matters were relevant. They did not, in the outcome letter available, demonstrate a source-by-source examination of how the patient's 2017 understanding had been represented, what had happened to his bedside objection, or whether severe psychological danger had received its own protective response.

The subsequent 2026 identification of pathogenic PRKN variants and the attribution of Parkinsonism and dystonia to Parkin disease deepen the retrospective question. They do not allow 2026 knowledge to be invented in the minds of clinicians in 2017. They do not establish that every corn or burning sensation was caused by dystonia. They establish that the Parkinsonian and dystonic components belonged to a disease process whose lower-limb phenotype deserves reconstruction. In 2017 the neurologist had already considered PARK2/PRKN and had already observed dystonic-looking movement. The mature question is what proportion of the longstanding foot and gait presentation was adequately characterised before the painful-foot territory received a relatively separate documentary status.

This is why the patient commissions the examination in October 2026. He does not have to deny the correct diagnosis, effective treatment or the reality of medication risk. He does not have to prove a straight causal chain from one bedside encounter to every later injury. His accusation is directed at an institutional account that became more extensive without demonstrably returning to the original unfinished relationship. **He says he identified the omission on the eighth day. Recording that objection would have made the disagreement visible to later clinicians and reviewers. Instead, the completed record gave the unresolved feet a place within a coherent management framework and did not visibly preserve the Protest. Eight years later he was still requiring the institution to explain what had happened to the question he first raised.**

## 1. What the GP sent and what the hospital received

The full 29 June 2017 GP letter is the proper starting source for understanding what the hospital was asked to do. It was a request for urgent medical assessment of deterioration in motor function, but the feet were integral to the history. The GP described a patient whose gait had seemed stiff and festinant when he first attended with bilateral foot issues. The patient had initially attributed his gait difficulty to calluses and corns, while the GP subsequently detected neurological signs which made that explanation insufficient. Increasing tone, difficulty initiating movement, toe clawing and inability to relax combined with worsening manual dexterity and the possibility of losing employment. The purpose of the referral was to look beyond any single obvious foot lesion. The whole presentation required a neurological opinion.

This makes the case more complex than a complaint that a referral was ignored. The hospital responded to the suspected neurological condition and established that the GP's concern was well founded. The distinction concerns the relation between what was discovered and the problem that had prompted discovery. A reader may reasonably regard severe Parkinsonism as the central urgent finding. But identifying the most serious disease does not automatically complete the assessment of every manifestation through which it may have presented. A diagnosis can be accurate and still incomplete as an explanation of the patient's disability. That is especially important where the patient has both abnormal movement and painful structural changes in the same part of the body.

The hospital's abbreviated referral section is instructive. It focused on difficulty initiating movement, increased tone, stiffness and cogwheeling. Those were clinically important, but the phrasing made the investigation appear principally a search for the neurological cause of general movement dysfunction. The original calluses, corns and clawing toes were no longer foregrounded in that initial summary. The patient had become, documentarily, a person referred for abnormal movement more than a person whose abnormal movement and painful feet were linked in an unresolved presentation. This is not proof of improper summarisation. It is an observable change in emphasis with possible consequences for what clinicians regarded as the outstanding task.

The difference between information loss and information demotion matters. The feet did not vanish entirely from the record. Neurology examined and described them; Podiatry was engaged. But recorded facts can lose their relationship to one another without being removed. A summary can accurately preserve a list of symptoms while diminishing the question that made their coexistence clinically meaningful. In this case, the clinical relationship among lower-limb posturing, toe clawing, abnormal loading and pain needed an owner. The documents more clearly identify who dealt with Parkinsonism and who dealt with structural foot care than who would determine whether one process contributed to the other.

In a hospital treating an unusual movement disorder, the clinician does not need to identify one universal cause. Mixed mechanisms are entirely possible. A fixed toe deformity may produce a pressure lesion; a peripheral disorder may cause burning; Parkinsonian rigidity or dystonia may influence gait, posture and the pressure placed on particular parts of the foot. These are not mutually exclusive explanations. The problem is that a division between services can be mistaken for proof of separation between mechanisms. A referral to Podiatry may be entirely appropriate and still insufficient as an answer to the neurological question. A neurologist can identify dystonia and still leave its distribution, prominence and contribution to pain inadequately characterised.

The patient first encountered the consequences of this distinction through care, not through document analysis. His symptoms were physically present. He could see and feel the feet; he could describe their discomfort and the way they behaved. The hospital had clinical records showing that the feet mattered. On the stipulated account, he asked to have that part of his illness properly examined before he left. It was the simplest possible audit question: which part of the original problem has not actually been dealt with?

## 2. The eighth-day opportunity

The date of the Protest matters because opportunities had accumulated. Monday 3 July was the first day of admission. The eighth calendar day was Monday 10 July. That period included a weekend and should not be inflated into eight complete days of consultant examination. Yet it encompassed a substantial specialist investigation. By the time discharge approached, the clinical team had had opportunities to review the referral, question the patient, examine his gait, identify the abnormal movement, consider specialist input and communicate a working formulation. The patient was not bringing an entirely new symptom on the morning he was due to leave. He was questioning the handling of symptoms that were present from the start.

The neurological examination had identified a dystonic-looking lower limb. Dystonia describes involuntary muscle contractions or postures that can twist, pull or position a body part abnormally. It is relevant to feet because toes may curl, feet may turn or pressure distribution may change during walking and standing. That does not mean dystonia explained every painful lesion. It does mean that the examination had generated information capable of bearing directly on the original complaint. The record also described pes planus, hammertoes and abnormal gait. These details were not remote possibilities. They concerned the same anatomical region in which the patient experienced pain.

Neurology's prescription of clonazepam for leg tension reinforced that the lower limbs were an active treatment target. The completed account contemplated whether the longer-term procyclidine plan might alter the need for clonazepam. In other words, the patient already possessed symptoms the specialist regarded as neurologically treatable. At the same time, his feet were routed to structural assessment and possible peripheral explanations. Such a division might have been appropriate. What was missing from the surviving reasoning was a sufficiently explicit account of why these domains could safely be treated as separate, how their interaction would be monitored, and what new evidence would prompt a change in that conclusion.

The GP's 6 July call to Podiatry adds an unexpected detail. The GP did not necessarily forget the integrated question after the hospital admission. He sought priority foot care and identified the case as related to Parkinson's. This record prevents a simple story in which primary care originated an integrated presentation and thereafter completely accepted a separation. The GP remained an important carrier of the relationship. Whether the telephone call communicated enough detail to Podiatry is not established, but the record shows continuing attention to the feet as part of a neurological case. That strengthens the question of whether Neurology's completed account adequately assigned responsibility for integration.

On 10 July, the patient says he objected directly. For this account, the encounter is accepted as his first-hand evidence. Four medical students were reportedly present. That presence matters as a setting rather than as independently verified corroboration. A teaching round offers the supervising clinician an opportunity to demonstrate how a patient challenge is handled: listen to the specific concern, distinguish its factual and diagnostic elements, respond to what can be examined immediately, and explain what will be investigated later. Students need not recognise a clinical omission to witness how a doctor treats uncertainty. Nothing in the patient's account establishes that the students understood the medical record or agreed with his assessment. The educational significance lies in the kind of clinical decision being made before them.

The objection also marked a change in professional notice. Before it, the failure to integrate the feet could be attributed to compression, prioritisation or uncertainty. After it, the clinician had been told that the patient regarded the examination as unfinished. The doctor remained free to conclude that the assessment had been sufficient. But that conclusion would now need to be capable of answering the patient's specific concern. It would not be enough merely to repeat the Parkinsonian diagnosis, because the complaint did not deny that diagnosis. The patient was asking about what happened to the feet within the investigation.

What could have happened? The clinician could have re-examined the feet, reviewed the GP's original wording, checked whether foot posture changed with movement or rest, explained the possible interactions between neurological and mechanical findings, clarified the scope of Podiatry's assessment or recorded a reasoned outpatient plan. The right response was not necessarily another inpatient test. It was an accountable clinical disposition. A simple entry acknowledging the objection would have changed the audit trail, even if no additional inpatient examination had been appropriate. It would have recorded that the patient did not regard the inquiry as complete and that the clinical team had made a decision about what to do with that concern.

That entry is missing from the specialist account examined. The omission cannot prove that the conversation did not occur. Nor does it prove the clinician privately recognised an error. It does mean that the authoritative document did not carry forward the patient's account of the dispute. Later readers would see an extensive investigation and a treatment plan. They would not automatically see that the patient had, before discharge, challenged whether the feet had been adequately investigated. The omission removed a potential point of comparison between what the hospital said it had done and what the patient said was left undone.

## 3. The patient changed in the writing

The most exact evidence in this case lies in successive versions of the patient's supposed understanding. The handwritten interview of 3 July recorded his awareness that foot pain and leg tension were different. That could be entirely accurate. A patient who has felt a deep muscular tightness and a sharp pressure pain at a corn may describe them as different. A useful clinical history should preserve those experiences faithfully because they help define the symptoms needing explanation.

The typed history made a stronger claim: that they were “not the same problem.” The phrase is capable of an innocent reading. It may reflect a fuller consultation than the handwritten notes captured. It may be shorthand for different phenomenological complaints. But it is not an exact equivalent. In ordinary English, two different sensations may still belong to one medical problem, while two different medical problems imply some degree of causal separation. The phrase therefore contains interpretive force not necessarily present in the shorter source note.

The final impression then stated that the patient had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” his leg tension from the corns, calluses and painful feet. This wording matters for its strength and for its location. It is situated immediately after the speculation that the painful distal phenomena may have been completely separate or due to peripheral neuropathy, perhaps related to earlier alcohol use, followed by the qualification “Not clear.” The impression moves from clinical uncertainty to a stronger representation of the patient's personal separation. The patient becomes part of the apparent support for a division the clinical account itself has not established with certainty.

The difference is not a lawyer's quibble over synonyms. Medical reports routinely rely on patients' statements to distinguish diagnoses, infer mechanisms and choose investigations. If a patient's account of different feelings is represented as a conclusion about different medical problems, the later reader may see independent patient confirmation where the source actually supports only symptom differentiation. The final report can thereby become more persuasive without becoming more evidentially complete. The statement may be factually plausible in isolation while remaining materially misleading in the explanatory position it occupies.

The clinical alternatives were genuinely open. Structural pathology was present. A peripheral cause for burning foot pain was possible. The history of alcohol use could properly form part of differential consideration. The issue is not the mere mention of these possibilities. It is the difficulty of identifying the clinical bridge by which the neurological and painful-foot domains were divided and the associated question left without an explicit integrated follow-up task. The word “perhaps” acknowledges uncertainty about the mechanism. The more emphatic patient attribution may nevertheless make the separation appear settled from the patient's perspective.

That is why the reported Protest, although not used to prove a hidden drafting motive, makes the final account so difficult to read without concern. On the patient's account, he had just told the consultant the feet had not been adequately investigated. The final specialist synthesis did not visibly record that challenge. Instead, it described the patient as strongly separating the symptoms in his own mind. The two representations are not mutually exclusive: a patient may distinguish sensations while also insisting their causes need proper investigation. But placing only the first representation into the authoritative account leaves the second invisible. The document does not show that the patient had challenged the completeness of the work on which its final explanation rested.

An investigation of documentary integrity would ask who drafted the typed history, whether the original handwritten note was available at that stage, whether the final impression was dictated or revised after discharge, whether extra spoken history justified the stronger claim and whether the clinician had access to the original GP referral when formulating the separate/peripheral explanation. The completed specialist document includes a DaTscan addendum from 13 July, after the patient left hospital. That proves its finished form incorporated later information. It does not prove the foot paragraphs were drafted after the Protest or because of it. The documentary transformation is visible. Its particular motive and production chronology remain questions for the source history.

It is possible to describe the effect without claiming a conspiracy. The medical record created a more stable version of the patient's understanding than the earliest surviving notes justify on their face. That version could be read by other doctors, the patient himself and later reviewers. It carried authority because it appeared within a sophisticated specialist report. The record thus became more than a repository of the consultation. It became a source from which later people could learn what the patient allegedly knew about his own symptoms. That is a powerful responsibility. It calls for particular care when the attributed understanding supports a medical division that remains uncertain.

## 4. A completed account is not a completed investigation

The final specialist report is impressive in its consideration of Parkinsonism. It discusses severe young-onset disease, unusual signs, imaging, possible genetic causes, colleagues' views and the risks of treatment. It acknowledges that the prognosis and genetic explanations were not fully settled. It considers whether waiting on levodopa might protect the patient from dyskinesia and other adverse effects associated with commencing treatment unusually young. It also recognises the possibility that later treatment might prove beneficial and that deferral involved difficult judgment. A fair examination of the case must preserve that sophistication.

Yet a sophisticated account can be uneven. The amount of explanation devoted to Parkinsonian diagnosis and therapy is not matched by an equally detailed account of the relationship between the painful feet and the neurological motor findings. The specialist impression permitted the foot problem to be independent or neuropathic, but it did not show a comparably developed set of positive and negative findings demonstrating that separation. The completed plan addressed neurological treatment and follow-up, and the general discharge record identified Podiatry. What it did not clearly show was a named responsibility for reopening the integrated foot–gait–tone question if medication responses, posture changes or recurrent lesions suggested interaction.

This is the difference between an investigated uncertainty and an uncertainty that may remain because a sufficiently focused investigation has not been completed. Both may be expressed with the words “not clear.” They have different implications. An uncertainty remaining after a reasoned differential assessment may be an appropriate working position. An uncertainty maintained because an important relationship was not fully examined should ordinarily be represented as an outstanding task. The documentation available does not resolve which explanation best describes the feet. It does, however, make the source of the uncertainty much less explicit than the degree of neurological treatment planning.

The formal recommendation “As per above” incorporated the specialist reasoning into a practical pathway. Nothing about that phrase itself indicates misconduct. It shows that the account had become actionable. The patient could leave hospital with a diagnosis, medication plan, nurse contact information and follow-up arrangements. He could also leave with the painful-foot relationship medically unresolved. The question is whether the unresolved relationship remained an active question or had been normalised as a durable uncertainty that no one was explicitly required to reconstruct. It is possible to have an organised plan without an adequate answer to every component of the original referral.

The same distinction applies to the Complex Case Meeting. The report records that colleagues considered the unusual Parkinsonian presentation. That matters as evidence of genuine multidisciplinary reasoning. It does not establish, without the meeting notes, that the painful-foot relationship was specifically debated, that the lower-limb phenotype was characterised in the necessary detail or that the peripheral branch was supported by independent clinical evidence. A mention of a meeting is not a substitute for examining what was discussed. The same caution applies to outside consultation, which demonstrates resourcefulness but does not establish that every unresolved component reached the consulted specialist.

The investigation's completeness must therefore be measured against its own presenting object, not merely against whether the diagnosis of Parkinsonism was correct. The hospital did something important. It did not necessarily do everything the original integrated question required. The patient's eighth-day objection makes that discrepancy particularly significant because it was raised before the record's final account of the admission became authoritative. There was an opportunity to distinguish what had been discovered from what remained to be discovered. The written synthesis did not visibly preserve that opportunity as a disputed decision point.

## 5. What one missing entry would have changed

Suppose the clinician had written a straightforward contemporaneous note: the patient states that his feet have not been adequately investigated; neurological examination identifies Parkinsonism and dystonic-looking movement; structural lesions are present; the relationship of painful lesions to abnormal movement remains uncertain; the clinical team has discussed the options and arranged a specified assessment or follow-up. That entry would not establish negligence. It would not compel the conclusion that the feet were neurologically caused. It would not necessarily have justified an extended hospital stay. But it would have changed the documentary position irreversibly.

A later clinician would know that the patient's separation of sensations was not consent to leave the underlying relationship unexamined. A Podiatrist could see that the issue was not simply removing a painful corn but understanding whether neurological positioning or altered loading contributed to recurrent pressure. A movement-disorders clinician could recognise that the original lower-limb phenotype had an open causal question. A GP would have an explicit point of comparison when later symptoms changed. A complaint reviewer would know exactly when the patient challenged the investigation and what the hospital did in response. These consequences concern visibility and accountability, not guaranteed clinical outcomes.

Without the entry, the final report becomes the main surviving explanation of what happened in hospital. It shows examination, diagnosis, differential discussion and treatment. It does not show the patient identifying a substantial remaining concern. The subsequent reviewer must reconstruct the omission by comparing the GP referral, examination, handwritten notes and final synthesis. What could have been an ordinary recorded disagreement becomes a historical controversy requiring documentary forensics. That is a loss to the record's accuracy, even before one determines whether the original examination met an appropriate standard.

The absence is particularly significant because the final impression positively attributed a strong separation to the patient. The problem is not merely that a patient objection was omitted from a crowded document. It is that the record retained a patient-attributed proposition bearing upon the very relationship he says he wanted investigated. A later reader could infer acceptance of a separation where the patient's testimony establishes a challenge to the adequacy of examining it. Again, these positions are not logically incompatible. But a record faithful to the state of disagreement should make both visible. One cannot treat the first as sufficient representation of the patient while ignoring the second.

The loss of the Protest also mattered to later institutional accountability. An institution asked in 2025 why the feet were not properly reassessed could find a detailed neurological explanation already present in its own records. It could examine the reasonableness of treatment decisions and infer a coherent management history. Had the objection been recorded, the review would also have had a specific earlier decision to audit: what was the clinical disposition of an explicit challenge to the completeness of the investigation? Was the complaint heard? Was the original referral reconsidered? Was the patient examined or appropriately referred? Was responsibility allocated? A missing record entry can therefore narrow the questions future governance knows to ask.

This is the central practical claim of the manuscript. Recording the Protest would not have forced a different diagnosis, medication or outcome. It would have made the unfinished question visible to every later user of the record. The failure to preserve that point of disagreement did not make it clinically disappear; it made it harder to locate within the official history of care. The patient continued to carry the objection. The documents did not carry it with the same clarity.

## 6. Therapeutic success did not settle the foot question

After discharge, the patient experienced striking improvement. Procyclidine and other treatment altered motor function in ways he found transformative. Contemporary messages record his gratitude and a sense that movement once severely impaired had become possible again. This was not a patient who received no useful care or viewed his neurologist as an enemy from the outset. The relationship contained trust, continuity and real success. This matters because the documentary case is not dependent on portraying every medical encounter as deficient. A record can contain correct diagnoses and successful therapy while retaining an inadequately supported proposition about another part of the presenting problem.

Improvement may in fact complicate a defective explanation. If neurological medication improves leg tension and movement, the patient may reasonably regard the neurological diagnosis as established and the treatment as beneficial. That does not resolve whether some painful foot phenomena are independent, neurologically aggravated or mixed. A treatment response can be persuasive without being mechanistically specific. Analgesic experience, posture, muscle contraction and walking patterns can all change under treatment. It is precisely because neurological medication can affect several dimensions of function that the response must be interpreted carefully rather than assigned automatically to one diagnostic compartment.

The 2017 interruption of procyclidine is a central observation. The patient's messages described a deterioration in tension and walking when treatment was interrupted. The neurologist's April 2018 account later recorded the rapid reported return of a phenomenon at the outside left corn. The note's evidential status is exact: it is a clinician-authored record of the patient's report, not direct measurement of a new lesion forming. The patient reported a temporal relationship between withdrawal and focal foot experience. The event did not establish the lesion's mechanism. But it supplied a natural comparison between treatment states affecting the area previously assigned a possible separate explanation.

What would an integrated reassessment have considered? Whether the toes changed position when medication was active or wearing off; whether the lateral foot bore different weight; whether there was fixed structural pathology independent of motor state; whether the burning was neuropathic or mechanically provoked; whether pain fluctuated with dystonic muscle activity; and whether foot care and neurological medication needed coordinated evaluation. Such questions need not have led to a single cause. They could have produced a mixed model, perhaps describing primary neurological movement disorder with secondary mechanical pressure lesions and independent peripheral symptoms. Even an inconclusive but explicit reconstruction would have improved the record by identifying what had been tested.

The available notes do not demonstrate that a comprehensive reconstruction occurred in response to the 2018 observation. Treatment remained active. The patient improved at times and worsened at others. Podiatry dealt with real structural lesions. Clinicians recorded pain and abnormal movement. But the historical account continued to permit neuropathic or separate foot explanations. That may have remained clinically reasonable. The criticism is not that the word *neuropathic* should never have appeared. It is that the degree of renewed evidence supporting the original division must be distinguished from the repetition of a plausible category.

In medicine, repeated use of a label can make it appear independently confirmed. A first specialist writes that neuropathy is possible. A later clinician copies or paraphrases the term. The patient learns the term and may use it. Another service then sees “neuropathic pain” in the history. Unless the term is clearly distinguished from an established peripheral-nerve diagnosis, later readers can mistake the circulation of language for accumulating diagnostic proof. The canonical record contains fresh observations and fresh hypotheses as well as continuity, so the process was not pure copying. But it still invites a genealogy of the peripheral explanation: when was neuropathy a provisional mechanism, when was it a pain description and when, if ever, was disease-level neuropathy positively established?

The same applies to alcohol history. Past alcohol intake was a relevant factor to ask about. A possible alcohol-related neuropathy is not inherently an illegitimate differential. Its influence becomes problematic only if it receives more explanatory weight than the evidence justifies, or if later contradictory information fails to alter that weight. The patient's complaint alleged such anchoring. That allegation must be tested through the sequence of clinical notes, not accepted or rejected because an alcohol history appeared in 2017. The wording “perhaps” and “Not clear” originally reflected uncertainty. Later records should be examined for whether they preserved that qualification whenever neuropathy was discussed.

## 7. The recurring feet and the limits of local reassessment

The medical history after 2018 was not a blank interval. The GP continued to see abnormal gait, clawing feet and increasing tone. In 2019 primary-care correspondence reconnected these features, undermining an account in which all clinicians simply forgot the original integrated problem. Primary care repeatedly acted as a source of relational information. The question is what happened when that information returned to specialist authority. The GP could identify associations and request review; he could not automatically settle the specialist classification of dystonia, neuropathy or Parkinsonian pain.

The 2020 assessment of an apparent footdrop is important contrary evidence. Neurology did not merely inherit a label without examination. It concluded that the unusual foot movement was Parkinsonian rather than true peripheral footdrop. That was meaningful discrimination based on contemporary findings. It demonstrates that the clinical system could recognise neurological explanations for unusual distal movement and was prepared to revise a particular interpretation when warranted. This fact must remain in the account because it prevents a caricature of a completely frozen framework.

Yet an accurate local finding does not answer the original global question. Identifying a later foot movement as Parkinsonian does not necessarily establish whether earlier toe clawing contributed to pressure lesions. Recognising OFF-related pain does not necessarily reconstruct the evolution of structural abnormalities over many years. Adjusting medication to manage dyskinesia or morning pain does not necessarily correct the recorded claim that the patient had strongly separated foot pain and leg tension as different medical problems. The system may work intelligently inside particular episodes while leaving a foundational explanation intact.

In 2023 the record contained clinical reasoning about whether pain arose during an OFF state, whether the timing corresponded with Parkinsonian fluctuation, and whether peripheral mechanisms remained possible. These are genuine attempts to understand the changing condition. They weaken any claim of absolute diagnostic inertia. But the issue is whether they were gathered into an integrated phenotype review: what was present before 2017, what the GP had referred, how dystonia and gait influenced the feet, what procyclidine withdrawal showed, what subsequent movement findings revealed and how structural and neurological mechanisms interacted. The record shows elements of this inquiry, not a readily identifiable complete reconstruction.

The distinction matters for deciding what counts as correction. If a patient complains that a founding premise is wrong, an answer about how many medications were adjusted does not by itself establish that the premise was re-examined. An institution can provide elaborate evidence of adaptation while answering a narrower question than the one in dispute. The patient does not deny that treatment changed. He asks whether the explanation governing the clinical relationship changed in response to the information those treatments generated. That question is specific enough to be audited. One can locate the original premise, identify new evidence and search for a later note that explicitly re-derived or rejected the premise. The current collection does not show that event clearly.

The time dimension compounds the seriousness. A speculative separation may be clinically tolerable when first made. If years of new evidence repeatedly bear upon it, the distinction between reasonable provisional uncertainty and unexamined continuity becomes increasingly important. Each new episode does not necessarily require a complete diagnostic reset. But when an original integrated problem repeatedly generates observations across the very boundary that was drawn, responsibility for revisiting that boundary cannot remain permanently implicit. There should be a recognisable point at which the accumulated evidence is assessed together.

## 8. Young-onset Parkinsonism, dystonia and the significance of PRKN

The neurological diagnosis itself deserves respect. The patient's condition was unusual and severe at a young age. The treating neurologist investigated it carefully, considered different neurodegenerative possibilities and recognised a potential hereditary cause. The 2017 record refers to PARK2/PRKN as a live possibility and discusses the uncertainty and practical value of testing. It does not support a claim that Parkinsonism or dystonic-looking movement was simply missed. The difficult question is how completely the long lower-limb phenotype was characterised and how that understanding informed management.

PRKN-related disease is relevant because it can involve young-onset Parkinsonism and dystonia, including prominent lower-limb manifestations. The later 2026 genetic evidence identified pathogenic variants and a specialist attribution of Parkinsonism and dystonia to Parkin disease. It provides a biological endpoint for a hypothesis already entertained in the historical record. It does not establish what treatment was obligatory in 2017, nor does it prove the origins of every structural lesion or episode of pain. Retrospective expert judgment must assess what features were clinically observable and what competent specialist practice reasonably required at the time.

The more demanding question concerns predominance. Was dystonia a minor associated observation or a major manifestation of the long-standing lower-limb disorder? How much of the abnormal toe position, foot loading, gait and pain was dynamic? Which deformities were fixed? Were different symptoms independently generated or did neurological posturing aggravate mechanical pathology? The 2017 report noticed dystonic-looking gait but does not show a movement-disorders-level characterisation of these relationships at comparable resolution. If expert review finds that dystonia was a major component of the original clinical problem, the significance of the early separation could change materially.

The treatment history presents a further question. Starting levodopa unusually young can involve serious long-term trade-offs, including dyskinesia. Later complications in this record demonstrate that those risks were not fictional. A clinician might reasonably seek to defer or minimise exposure while using alternatives. But a treatment strategy is safest when the full target of treatment is understood. If disabling pain or dystonic posturing was an important manifestation of the disease, an assessment of medication risk should be balanced against the burden of those symptoms, not only against the conventional signs of Parkinsonism. That is an expert question, not a conclusion available from the later genetic result alone.

The patient has questioned whether an earlier trial or different management could have prevented years of suffering. The evidence cannot presently calculate such a counterfactual. Drug benefits fluctuate; side effects emerge; structural lesions may persist independently; disease progression and other causes affect pain. The eventual efficacy of Sinemet CR does not prove that the same treatment would have had the same balance of benefit and harm in 2017 or 2021. The record nevertheless contains enough medication-state information to justify a rigorous historical review of treatment targets and why different options were pursued or deferred.

## 9. The point where suffering required more than symptom management

By 2023 the documentary question was no longer confined to whether the foot pain had been assigned the right explanatory category. The patient was in profound distress. On 26 March the GP wrote urgently about severe bilateral foot and lower-leg pain, recording that it was “causing death wish.” He expressed concern about the patient's welfare and requested prompt specialist attention. That letter must be read as contemporaneous notice of a significant psychological-safety issue. It is not proof of an active suicidal plan, and the phrase does not automatically prescribe a particular emergency intervention. But it required clinicians to understand what danger the patient was describing and respond proportionately.

The neurological service did respond to the painful symptoms. Clinicians considered medication timing, OFF phenomena, neuropathic possibilities and treatment changes. The clinical record contains meaningful contact and reassessment. It would be inaccurate to say that extreme symptoms generated no response of any kind. Yet treatment of the presumed cause of suffering does not by itself establish that someone assessed the risk to the person expressing a death wish. Pain management and safety assessment are related but distinct duties. One can be pursued while the other remains insufficiently visible in the record.

A safety response, depending on the actual risk, could involve clarifying suicidal thoughts, intent and plans; reviewing protective factors and means of harm; considering urgent mental-health input; ensuring the patient had appropriate emergency contacts; coordinating GP and specialist responsibilities; making a follow-up arrangement; and documenting the rationale for what was or was not required. The particular form of response would depend on assessment. The reviewed material has not yielded a clear, distinct, coordinated account of that protective process. It may exist in records not supplied. That is precisely what a complete institutional response should determine rather than assuming that neurological review settles the question.

This later issue must not be presented as automatically caused by the wording of the 2017 discharge letter. That would collapse a long and medically complicated history into an unsupported chain. The patient's illness, structural pathology, medication effects, disability and changing circumstances all matter. The documentary relevance is different. The same medical system that had failed, on the patient's account, to preserve his first objection was now receiving explicit information about a dangerous level of suffering. The test of its record was once again whether a clinically significant statement would be represented accurately and translated into an accountable response.

The patient had lived through periods of improvement and worsening. This matters to the seriousness of the later warning. The death-wish language was not a continuous attribute of his personality inserted to make the old case more tragic. It entered the clinical record at a definite time, attached to severe pain and accompanied by the GP's concern. By 2025, the patient was again describing suffering he could not bear to continue. The human trajectory should be understood from the dated documents, not reconstructed backwards to give every earlier event a predetermined fatal consequence.

What becomes disturbing is the administrative normality of the sequence. An urgent referral arrives. A nurse sees the patient. Neurological medication is discussed. Clinic letters record symptoms. The system remains active. Yet a reviewer seeking a separate account of psychological risk and protection cannot readily locate it within the supplied sources. There may be good clinical reasons why one level of response was chosen over another. Those reasons should be documented and available for review. Without them, continuing ordinary care cannot itself demonstrate that extraordinary distress was adequately assessed.

The danger was new even if the underlying illness was old. A diagnosis made in 2017 could not discharge responsibility for a psychological-risk warning received in 2023. The patient was not merely a Parkinsonian case proceeding along a treatment pathway. He was a person whose tolerance of suffering had changed. A service could defend every historical medicine choice and still have a separate contemporary obligation to assess the patient's safety. That distinction should remain central to the institutional inquiry.

## 10. March 2025: the Protest returns in writing

On 19 March 2025 the patient transmitted a formal complaint titled *Diagnostic Anchoring, Bias, and Failure to Reassess (2017–2025)*. The canonical record preserves both the complaint text and the provenance of its email transmission. It was received or logged by Quality and Patient Safety on 25 March. The different dates are not inconsistent: one concerns sending and the other administrative receipt or registration. The original complaint requested an independent review, disclosure of records and internal correspondence, a written explanation, and preservation of relevant material. It approached the issue as one of accountability and learning rather than only an individual dispute about a prescription.

The March complaint contained the patient's own interpretation of the preceding eight years. He believed an alcohol-related neuropathy explanation had become fixed and inhibited investigation of Parkinsonian pain. He described treatment responses as contradicting the inherited framework. He questioned why the possibilities revealed by Sinemet CR had not been tested earlier. Those are claims made in a primary patient-authored complaint. They are direct evidence of what the institution was being asked to examine, but not independent proof that every prior clinician refused reconsideration or that medication relief excluded neuropathy. The subsequent specialist records contain meaningful contrary facts, including earlier neurological treatment, fresh assessment of foot movement and consideration of OFF pain.

The complaint's account of taking Sinemet CR independently also needs preserving without erasing the clinical chronology. The neurologist's later correspondence refers to his own prescription dated 10 March 2025 and explains that he had understood the medication to have been supplied through that plan. The patient described obtaining and beginning the medication himself and experiencing remarkable relief the following morning. Both accounts bear on communication, timing and the patient's experience of continuity of care. Their difference does not negate the relief he described; neither does it establish that the trial was unprescribed. The documentary task is to keep what each source actually says rather than choose whichever version best serves an allegation.

The complaint is a continuation of the bedside Protest in a particular sense. In July 2017 the patient had asked why the feet had not been properly dealt with. In March 2025 he was asking why the possibility of a neurological contribution had not been sufficiently re-evaluated as his condition and treatment responses changed. The allegation had become more developed. It now contained claims about diagnostic anchoring, avoidable harm and institutional learning. But its documentary root remained the question of how the feet had been represented and what evidence was used to decide their relationship to the Parkinsonian disorder. The 2017 objection supplied the original missing decision point; the 2025 complaint sought accountability for what followed.

There is a practical difference between the two moments. The bedside Protest offered the possibility of contemporaneous clinical correction. The formal complaint offered the possibility of retrospective reconstruction. In 2017 the hospital could examine, explain or assign further investigation. In 2025 the institution could compare the original referral with the specialist note, distinguish patient language from clinician interpretation, examine the diagnostic genealogy of neuropathy, trace medication-state observations and audit the response to the 2023 safety warning. The later process did not need to solve all medical causation questions to determine whether the documentation was accurate and the major concerns had been examined.

The complaint's central value is therefore not whether its author had a perfect clinical theory. Patients can be wrong about mechanisms and right about missing information. A complaint alleging diagnostic anchoring may contain overstatements while exposing a genuine failure to document the basis of a decision. The question for independent review is not whether every proposition in the complaint survives expert scrutiny. It is whether the institution identified the proper objects of investigation and tested them against source records rather than treating the complaint as another item to be reconciled with an existing clinical story.

## 11. The institutional account of the institutional account

The April 2025 Quality and Patient Safety correspondence characterised the complaint as concerning clinical judgment, particularly diagnostic anchoring and pain management. It referred the matter through an appropriate clinical-judgment pathway. That procedural step was not inherently improper. The hospital was entitled to seek an explanation from the clinicians involved. A treating neurologist's account can be indispensable historical evidence. The danger lies in treating that account as equivalent to independent testing of how the record's original categories were created.

The neurologist's response described genuine care. It explained reluctance to increase Sinemet rapidly because of dyskinesia and other risks. It recognised that Sinemet could make feet feel better from a pain perspective even when the original cause of pain was not itself dopaminergic. It referred to pregabalin and other treatment attempts. It expressed pleasure at the patient's relief, regret for his suffering and a sense of a longstanding constructive relationship. These passages matter because they show care was active, treatment risks were real and the clinician was not simply denying that medication changed the experience of pain.

The response also illustrates the documentary problem. A clinician can explain why his treatment decisions were reasonable within the framework he used. He may be entirely sincere and may provide an accurate account of those decisions. But such an explanation does not independently establish that the foundational attribution of symptom separation to the patient was faithful, that the foot–gait–tone relationship was sufficiently investigated or that later observations were assembled into a revised model. The original author is an important witness to his own reasoning. He cannot, solely by explaining his practice, supply the independent provenance audit that a challenge to the original record requires.

On 3 September 2025 the Serious Incident Management Team issued an outcome following presentation of a Preliminary Assessment Report on 27 August. The multidisciplinary team concluded that decisions at Galway University Hospitals had been appropriate and that there was no deficit in care. The communicated reasons emphasised risk factors in determining when to start Sinemet, the consideration of alternative diagnoses and the fact that Sinemet had first been prescribed in 2021. These are significant aspects of the case. They address a narrower treatment-history question strongly. They do not, in the outcome letter, show that the institution reconstructed the source-to-synthesis transformation in 2017, examined the bedside Protest or audited the later safety response.

The distinction must be exact. The outcome letter is not the entire Preliminary Assessment Report. It is possible that the underlying review examined more than the summary communicated to the patient. A judgment that the institution never considered the documentary issues would require those materials. Yet the letter does not itself establish that the original allegation received a complete answer. Its reasoning is concentrated on clinical management within the inherited categories. The question raised by the Protest concerns whether those categories were adequately made and revised. One cannot infer the accuracy of the foundation from the defensibility of decisions subsequently taken upon it.

This is where an authoritative medical record may become involved in validating itself. The specialist account creates a category. Later letters employ or refine that category. Treatment decisions become documented within it. A retrospective institutional review sees a coherent chronology of diagnosis, treatment and caution about risks. The resulting continuity may be real and clinically important. But it is not independent proof that the first separation was justified. Consistency of a record is not the same thing as independent corroboration of a claim within it. The proper review must go back to the original sources, including the GP referral and handwritten history, and test what the later specialist account added.

The September decision also requires separation from the psychological-safety inquiry. Finding that Sinemet was not prescribed earlier because of genuine concerns about adverse effects does not establish that death-wish language in March 2023 was assessed and managed appropriately. Nor does later successful pain relief provide retrospective assurance that the patient was protected while the pain was severe. These matters require their own evidence. An institution answering the treatment question may reach an entirely defensible conclusion and still leave another important question unanswered.

## 12. What institutional capability changes—and what it does not

The admission took place in a university teaching hospital with neurological expertise. The contemporary record identifies multidisciplinary discussion and clinical resources. Historical research into Galway's neurological environment indicates active engagement with Parkinsonism and unusual movement presentations, including dystonic foot phenomena. That setting is relevant because the patient had a complicated presentation requiring specialist interpretation. It shows the possibility of informed consultation and a structured response. It does not establish that any named individual was personally involved or available to the ward on 10 July.

Institutional capability cannot be proved by listing prominent doctors without verifying their actual roles. Relevant people include the treating neurologist, consultants consulted in the specific case, specialist nurses and clinicians responsible for foot assessment. The source record mentions a Complex Case Meeting, but its substantive notes have not been supplied in a form that establishes what was debated. A generic claim that the department possessed movement-disorders expertise is weaker than a dated record showing that this patient's dystonic foot phenotype was discussed, examined and assigned a reasoned plan. That is the evidence an institutional account should produce if it maintains that the relationship was properly handled.

Teaching status adds another dimension. The patient's report of four students at the Protest places the encounter in a setting where the clinical handling of uncertainty was also an educational act. The students were not responsible for determining diagnosis. Their presence does not establish wrongdoing. But a specialist responding to a patient's concern in front of trainees can demonstrate the difference between clinical authority and responsiveness. The legitimate authority of the clinician rests not on never being questioned but on being able to explain what has been established, recognise what has not and decide what follows. The patient says he presented exactly that kind of question.

An eight-day admission should not be judged by a mechanical count of days. It should be judged by what the hospital knew, what it had done and what decisions remained open. By 10 July the abnormal feet were known; the dystonic-looking lower limb was known; neurological tension was being treated; Podiatry had been engaged; and discharge was imminent. The objection therefore came when the service could compare its extensive neurological achievements with the still-unsettled relation to the painful feet. The patient's testimony makes the relevant opportunity concrete. The missing evidence is not proof that the whole investigation failed, but proof that the response to the outstanding relational question was sufficient.

## 13. The consequences a correct record could have made possible

The most important counterfactual is documentary before it is therapeutic. Had the Protest been recorded faithfully, later readers would have seen that the patient challenged the adequacy of the foot assessment before discharge. That fact could have prevented the patient's attributed separation of symptoms from being mistaken for acceptance that the clinical relationship required no further investigation. It would have given future clinicians an explicit reason to review what had been examined and what remained to be explained.

It might also have changed referrals. A Podiatry assessment could have been framed as part of a coordinated inquiry into pressure lesions and abnormal neurological loading. A movement-disorders review could have been asked to characterise toe clawing, gait, foot posture and pain together. Clinical correspondence might have specified the findings that would require renewed neurological assessment. None of these outcomes follows automatically from a note. But all become easier to request, coordinate and audit when the record accurately identifies the unresolved question and its origin.

In 2018 the procyclidine-withdrawal observation could have been read against that explicit unresolved relationship. The same applies to the 2019 GP correspondence, the 2020 Parkinsonian foot movement and later OFF-related pain. A clinician seeing the original objection could have understood those observations as information potentially bearing upon an outstanding causal question rather than only as discrete episodes needing local management. The effect of proper documentation would have been to preserve a diagnostic question across changes in treatment and personnel.

An accurate record could also have improved the patient's own understanding. The neurologist later described clinical letters as resources for patients and bridges between what clinicians intended and what patients understood. Such documents have educational force. If a letter presents the patient as having already made a strong separation of symptoms, he may be invited to adopt that framework even when the original clinical relationship remains uncertain. A record that explicitly preserved his concern would have shown a different state of knowledge: the patient experienced distinct sensations, the clinician was evaluating possible mechanisms, and the relationship remained a task for further investigation. That account would not have solved the disease. It would have avoided presenting the patient as more certain than the available source justified.

A more complete record might have led to different treatment decisions. It might have prompted earlier recognition of mixed neurological and mechanical mechanisms. It might have improved coordination between Neurology and Podiatry. But these are possibilities requiring expert testing, not outcomes that can be asserted as inevitable. Medication choices were complicated by genuine risks. Foot lesions could remain painful for independent reasons. Later improvement cannot be transported backward unchanged. The counterfactual must remain disciplined: documentation can create conditions for better decisions without guaranteeing those decisions or their consequences.

The institutional counterfactual is firmer. A recorded Protest would have given the 2025 review a specific, dated event to examine. The team could have asked why the concern was raised, what was already known, what examination or explanation followed and how the discharge report represented the patient thereafter. The review could have compared the original GP referral, handwritten note, typed history and final impression as a provenance sequence rather than only reading the finished specialist account as an established historical source. The absence of the entry did not prevent a diligent reviewer from undertaking that reconstruction. It made the need for reconstruction less obvious from the routine record.

The safety counterfactual is separate. An accurate record of the 2017 Protest would not guarantee adequate suicide-risk assessment in 2023. The psychological-risk warning imposed its own contemporary responsibility. Still, a system accustomed to preserving patient objections and their disposition may be better positioned to ensure that later expressions of serious danger also become auditable clinical events. The common principle is not that all harms originate in one missing sentence. It is that clinically significant patient communication must not lose its force when translated into the institution's normal documentary categories.

## 14. The questions an independent review must actually answer

The review must distinguish three different questions: whether Parkinsonism was appropriately recognised and treated; whether the painful-feet, posture, tone and gait relationship was adequately assessed and followed through; and whether the patient's explicit challenge to the completeness of that assessment was heard, acted upon and faithfully recorded. An affirmative answer to the first cannot, without reasoning, stand in for answers to the second and third.

**The originating clinical task.** The 29 June 2017 GP referral described painful feet and lesions alongside toe clawing, altered gait, stiffness and increasing tone. The reviewer should identify where that integrated presentation was considered, what examination or specialist input addressed its interacting mechanisms, and what clinical rationale supported any decision to investigate the components separately. Recognition of severe Parkinsonism and referral to Podiatry are relevant evidence of care, but neither alone resolves the relationship.

**The eighth-day decision point.** On the patient's first-hand account, he challenged the adequacy of the foot investigation on 10 July 2017, in the presence of four students. The review should seek any ward entries, contemporaneous communications and available witness accounts, identify the clinician's response if ascertainable, and determine whether a further examination, explanation, referral or assigned follow-up occurred. The account is evidence to assess, not a finding of deliberate concealment. Equally, lack of an existing hospital entry should not be treated as proof that the objection never happened.

**The representation of the patient.** The handwritten distinction between foot pain and leg tension, the typed formulation that they were “not the same problem,” and the final attribution that the patient had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” the two require comparison. The reviewer should establish what each phrase was intended to mean, whether further history justified the stronger wording, when the relevant passages were composed, and whether the completed account accurately preserved the patient's concern that the relationship remained insufficiently investigated. The post-discharge DaTscan addendum does not itself establish when the foot passages were drafted.

**The record-keeping question.** A patient's accurate distinction between two sensations is not necessarily an agreement that their causes are unrelated. Nor must the patient prove the eventual diagnosis wrong before an explicit objection to the completeness of an investigation warrants appropriate consideration. The relevant professional record-keeping duties, as applicable in July 2017, and the reasonable clinical response in these circumstances require independent identification and assessment; this manuscript does not declare a breach merely from the absence of a surviving note. The reviewer should state what, if anything, should have been documented about the objection, its assessment and its disposition.

**The later tests.** The review should trace the 2018 medication-withdrawal foot phenomenon, later movement and OFF-state observations, and the 2025 response to Sinemet CR without treating medication response as proof that all corns, calluses or foot pain were neurological. It should explain whether and how the original causal formulation was revisited, rather than merely catalogue adjustments to symptomatic treatment.

**Safety and institutional scrutiny.** The March 2023 GP warning that pain was causing a death wish requires a separate audit of assessment, communication, referral and safety planning, including records not yet supplied. The March 2025 complaint and September 2025 SIMT outcome require comparison against the full Preliminary Assessment Report and review materials. The reviewer should identify whether the founding representation, the Protest, the unresolved relationship and the psychological-safety question were individually tested, rather than infer completeness from a conclusion of “no deficit in care.”

These are answerable questions, not demands for a predetermined finding. An adequate independent review may vindicate aspects of the original clinical decisions; it may identify missing records or legitimate reasons for separating mechanisms. What it should not do is substitute the success of one diagnostic task for a reasoned account of another task the patient says he explicitly raised.

## Conclusion: the question that should have remained visible

On Monday, 10 July 2017, the patient was on the eighth calendar day of a substantial neurological admission. The hospital had identified severe Parkinsonism and begun treatment. It had documented structural foot abnormalities and dystonic-looking lower-limb movement. It had received a GP referral in which painful feet, clawing toes, abnormal gait and increasing tone formed one unresolved clinical presentation. The patient says he told the neurologist that the feet had not been adequately investigated. He recalls four students present and reports that the requested examination did not follow.

The completed specialist record did not visibly preserve that objection. It preserved a sophisticated neurological synthesis and an uncertain possible separation of the painful feet. In that same account, the patient's original distinction between sensations acquired greater force as an attributed distinction between problems. The record gave the unresolved question a place within a coherent management plan. It did not show a comparably clear obligation to reconstruct the relationship that the patient had challenged.

The subsequent history was neither uniformly neglectful nor uniformly reassuring. Neurological treatment brought real relief. Withdrawal and medication changes produced new information. Foot movements and painful symptoms were repeatedly reassessed. The patient trusted his neurologist. Structural disease was genuine. Medication risks became serious. Yet the record does not clearly show the foundational foot–tone–gait relationship being re-derived in the light of the accumulated evidence. Local clinical adaptation was visible; global explanatory revision was less so.

By 2023, the record contained an urgent GP warning that severe pain was causing a death wish. By 2025, the patient was reporting intolerable suffering and demanding institutional review. The hospital's September decision defended the adequacy of care, especially the historical treatment strategy. The communicated reasons did not demonstrate a complete answer to the original documentary questions or the distinct psychological-safety issue. Additional review material may change that assessment, but the outcome letter alone does not settle it.

The governing counterfactual is modest and profound. **If the patient's Protest had been accurately recorded and answered, the unresolved relationship would have remained visible as a question the hospital knew it had been asked.** The record would show whether the feet were examined, why further investigation was unnecessary or who would take responsibility. Later clinicians would know that a patient who could distinguish foot pain from leg tension had nevertheless challenged the adequacy of the investigation into their relationship. Institutional reviewers would have a dated decision point against which to test the care that followed.

Recording the Protest would not have supplied an immediate cure. It would not have determined the proper medication. It would not have guaranteed that later suffering was avoidable. It would have prevented the hospital's finished explanation from being the only visible account of whether the original clinical question had been satisfactorily answered.

In October 2026, the patient is still asking the hospital to account for what happened when he raised that question. The 19 March 2025 complaint was another attempt to make it visible. This manuscript is another. The issue is not whether the institution can produce a coherent history of treatment. It can. The issue is whether that coherent history faithfully preserved the patient, the clinical relationship he asked it to investigate, the evidence that subsequently tested its assumptions and the danger that emerged as his suffering worsened.

**“I told you in 2017 that you had not dealt with my feet. Had you recorded that objection and what you did about it, the unfinished investigation would have been visible to every subsequent clinician and reviewer. Instead, the completed record preserved an explanation under which the question could remain unresolved. Eight years later, I was still asking you to answer it.”**

The patient is not asking for sympathy in place of evidence. He is asking that the institution's most authoritative documents be held to the standards of accuracy, openness and accountability that their authority requires.
